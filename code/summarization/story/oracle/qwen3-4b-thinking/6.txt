Memories serve as Willard’s only tether to Earth and humanity in his profound isolation, acting as both solace and torment. After his friend Dobbin’s death, Willard desperately attempts to "rip the memories of Earth out of his heart" to alleviate loneliness, but they remain irrevocably intertwined with his identity. These memories become his sole connection to a world he can never return to—a place defined by sensory details like the "crunch of snow," the "soft slap of rainwater," and the voices of loved ones. They sustain him through years of despair, fueling fleeting hope when he glimpses the Ghost Ship or recalls Earth’s beauty. Yet they also intensify his anguish, as the "horrible fantasies of terror" implanted by solitude distort reality. Crucially, memories become the lens through which Willard understands the story’s central paradox: the Ghost Ship phenomenon.
