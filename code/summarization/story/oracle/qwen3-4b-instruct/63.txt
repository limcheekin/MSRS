Farquar serves as a critical foil to the narrative’s exploration of morality, power, and intellectual conflict. His aggressive opposition to the Thinkers—particularly their perceived deception and reliance on illusion—embodies a reactive, violent form of scientific resistance rooted in distrust and personal grievance. Unlike the Thinkers, who represent rational, systemic, and morally grounded solutions through advanced mental science, Farquar operates from a position of ideological extremism, fueled by resentment over personal experiences (such as his failed relationship with Miss Arkady) and a belief that the world needs more action than truth. His actions—such as orchestrating veiled attacks on Maizie and threatening violence against the Thinkers—highlight the dangers of unchecked emotion in intellectual discourse. Yet, Opperly’s counterpoint reveals that Farquar’s rage is not just personal but symptomatic of a broader societal fear: the belief that power must be seized through force rather than evolved through reason. This tension underscores the story’s central theme—that morality in a dystopian future depends not on brute power or deception, but on the capacity for self-reflection and the acceptance of uncertainty. Farquar’s presence thus challenges the narrative’s moral framework, forcing the reader to confront whether true progress comes from confrontation or from the quiet, enduring pursuit of truth.
