Martin Kesserich copes with the loss of Mary Alice by maintaining a deep, obsessive love for her, which he channels into his scientific work and lifelong dedication to preserving their future. He has built a life centered around her memory, meticulously planning their shared future—such as trips to Buenos Aires, sailing down the inland passage, and teaching her Hungarian—year by year. This intense emotional focus allows him to live in a state of perpetual anticipation, believing that their future will one day be realized. His love is so profound and all-consuming that it becomes a central part of his identity and purpose. Even after her death in a railway accident in 1933, he continues to live as if she is still present, reinforcing the idea that his life is a continuous act of devotion and remembrance. The story reveals that his coping mechanism is not just grief, but a form of living in a constructed reality where Mary Alice remains central—her memory, her future, and their shared dreams sustained through meticulous planning and emotional dedication.
