In *The Girls from Earth*, societal expectations about women are deeply rooted in gender roles and colonial dynamics, creating a tension between personal desire and rigid norms. Women on Earth are expected to remain in domestic, passive roles—waiting for marriage, conforming to puritanical standards, and fulfilling societal duties like maintaining morality and family life. This is evident in Phyllis’s frustration with unfulfilled expectations and her sense of invisibility in a society that values marriage over independence. Meanwhile, the colonial world presents an alternative: women are encouraged—or compelled—to leave Earth and embrace a new identity as colonists, where they can work, build families, and achieve autonomy. The story reveals that these expectations are not just cultural but systemic—women are economically and socially marginalized on Earth, leading to a sense of disenfranchisement. The colonial solution, though presented as progressive, is underpinned by manipulation: women are offered a choice between emigration and severe penalties, highlighting how societal norms are enforced through coercion and economic pressure. Ultimately, the story critiques the gendered division of labor and the false promise of freedom in colonization, showing how both worlds impose constraints—Earth through stagnation and expectation, and the colonies through idealized, transactional roles. Women’s personal desires for love, security, and self-determination are suppressed by the prevailing societal structures, revealing a world where gender roles are not just social customs, but enforced realities.
