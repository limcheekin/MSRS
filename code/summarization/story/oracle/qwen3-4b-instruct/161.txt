The new world order and the Equalization Laws in Robert Silverberg’s *Master of Life and Death* emerge as a dystopian response to Earth’s critical population crisis in the 23rd century. With the global population reaching seven billion and resources dwindling, humanity adopts a rigid, technocratic system to manage overpopulation through enforced demographic control. The Equalization Laws mandate the elimination of individuals deemed "subnormal" or genetically unsound—particularly children with inherited conditions like tuberculosis—via euthanasia (known as "Happysleep"). Adults with degenerative diseases or disabilities are also sterilized or allowed to die prematurely. These policies are justified as necessary for the survival of the species, with the belief that future generations must be free from suffering and genetic defects. The Bureau of Population Equalization (Popeek) enforces these laws, with administrators like Roy Walton wielding immense power to decide who lives or dies. However, the system is deeply flawed, as it dehumanizes individuals, erases personal agency, and risks moral collapse when faced with exceptions—such as Walton’s decision to save Philip Prior’s child. This single act of defiance reveals the inherent contradiction at the heart of the new world order: while it claims to preserve life, it ultimately sacrifices human dignity and individual value in the name of population control.
