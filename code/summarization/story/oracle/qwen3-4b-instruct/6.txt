Memories hold profound significance in the story as the emotional anchor for Willard, the lone space traveler grappling with loneliness and existential despair. Throughout his decades-long isolation aboard the *Mary Lou*, memories of Earth—of walking on snow-covered streets, hearing familiar voices, and experiencing warmth and human connection—become the only true sources of meaning and hope. These memories sustain him through years of solitude, allowing him to maintain a sense of self and purpose even as reality fades around him. The haunting appearance of the Ghost Ship, initially dismissed as a hallucination, is later interpreted through the lens of memory and longing: it symbolizes the enduring presence of Earth and the human need for belonging. Willard’s belief in the Ghost Ship emerges not from superstition, but from the deep psychological imprint of memory, which distorts perception and fuels hope. Ultimately, the story reveals that memories are not just recollections—they are lifelines. When Willard finally realizes that he and the *Mary Lou* are now "ghosts" in space—no longer detectable to Earth ships—the final irony is that his most vivid, cherished memories of Earth have been the very thing that kept him alive. Thus, memories become both a source of suffering and a form of immortality, proving that even in the vast silence of space, human connection persists through the mind.
