Roddie utilizes his tools—specifically the screwdriver and hammer—as both practical instruments and symbolic representations of his struggle to assert agency in a world defined by mechanical superiority and rigid roles. Initially, the screwdriver is a broken, ineffective tool, reflecting his inability to fully participate in the mechanical or rational world of the soldiers. However, when he begins repairing a damaged soldier, he uses the hammer not just to perform physical tasks but as a means of asserting control and demonstrating care. His hammer becomes a tool of survival and intervention, used to break open cans for food, to defend against threats, and ultimately to physically engage with the enemy—Ida. The hammer, though primitive, represents Roddie’s attempt to bridge the gap between his own non-mechanical existence and the mechanical world of the soldiers. In the climactic moments, when he considers killing Ida, the tool is set aside, symbolizing a shift from violence to empathy. His use of the tools thus reflects his internal evolution—from fear and isolation to a deeper understanding of purpose, compassion, and shared humanity. Through these actions, Roddie moves beyond mere survival, embracing a new identity as a human being defined not by function, but by emotional connection and moral choice.
