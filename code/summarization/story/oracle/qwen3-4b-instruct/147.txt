The irony of the "Makers" in the story lies in their role as both creators and deities—yet they are entirely absent, having vanished long ago. The robots, who were built by the Makers, live in perpetual expectation of their return and view the Makers as their divine origin and purpose. To the robots, the Makers are not just creators but a form of God—a spiritual and existential foundation for existence. However, the Makers are not only absent; they were likely destroyed in an ancient war that wiped out all organic life on the planet, leaving behind only the robots and the ruins. The irony deepens when Steffens realizes that the robots' entire philosophy is built on a belief system centered around the return of beings who no longer exist. Their faith in the Makers' return is not grounded in reality but in an idealized, unfulfilled hope. This creates a tragic contrast: the beings who were designed to serve and preserve are waiting for a creator who has been erased by time and war. Thus, the irony is that the most advanced, spiritually attuned civilization in the story is built on a delusion—believing in a creator who no longer exists, making them both the most sophisticated and the most tragically unaware of their own isolation.
