The trip back in the story symbolizes a return to authenticity, simplicity, and emotional grounding after exposure to the rigid, impersonal, and often absurd rules of a highly regulated, universal society. Michael’s journey to Portyork—a cosmopolitan, tabu-laden world where every action is governed by strict customs—reveals the dissonance between societal conformity and personal identity. The constant pressure to adhere to tabus, from clothing and speech to dining and relationships, highlights the loss of individual freedom and emotional depth. When Michael realizes that concepts like marriage, personal desire, and familial choice are either forbidden or deeply compromised, he experiences a profound sense of alienation. His decision to return to the Brotherhood reflects a rejection of artificial harmony in favor of genuine human connection, simplicity, and the natural rhythms of life. The trip back is not merely physical but existential: it signifies the recognition that true belonging lies not in universal conformity, but in embracing one's own values, instincts, and primal bonds—values that the complex, sanitized society of Portyork systematically suppresses. Thus, the return embodies a return to self, to authenticity, and to a life rooted in human nature rather than societal mandates.
