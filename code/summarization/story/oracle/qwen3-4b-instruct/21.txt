Herbert's wife is Mrs. Herbert Hyrel, a central figure whose presence underscores the story's exploration of power, control, and the illusion of escape. She holds significant symbolic weight as the embodiment of stability and reality in contrast to Herbert’s desperate, self-destructive fantasies of freedom. While Herbert views her as a source of oppression—haunted by her wealth, indifference, and emotional dominance—she is, in fact, the one who exercises real control over the narrative. Her actions reveal a cold, calculated intelligence: she uses the telovis to escape reality, just as Herbert does, but she ultimately manipulates the system to subvert his plans. When she discovers his intention to kill her, she calmly and methodically turns the tables, using the telporter suit not to flee, but to dominate him—by reactivating his own device and rendering him unconscious. Her actions expose the irony of their shared dependency on technology: both are trapped in illusions, yet only she chooses to reclaim agency. In this grim tale, her significance lies not in passive suffering, but in active control, illustrating how true escape is not from reality, but from self-deception. She represents the quiet, enduring strength required to survive in a world where desperation breeds madness.
