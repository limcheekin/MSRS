The Kumaji's role in the story is central to exploring themes of betrayal, survival, and the complexities of homecoming. As the indigenous people of the desert, they represent the traditional, resistant force that opposes the encroaching Earth colony. Their conflict with the colonists symbolizes the tension between cultural survival and colonial expansion. The Kumaji are not merely antagonists—they embody the consequences of historical trauma and displacement, making their actions rooted in survival rather than pure malice. The story's exploration of betrayal is embodied in Tobias Whiting’s treachery, but the Kumaji’s presence forces Steve to confront moral ambiguity: should he punish a traitor or protect the innocent? The old Kumaji who remains in the village, despite being isolated and dying, chooses to stay because he sees his home as irreplaceable. His quiet acceptance of death contrasts with Whiting’s self-serving betrayal, highlighting that true homecoming is not about returning to a place, but about understanding one’s roots and accepting the cost of survival. Through this, the story reveals that homecoming is both emotional and ethical—a journey of reconciliation, not just physical return.
