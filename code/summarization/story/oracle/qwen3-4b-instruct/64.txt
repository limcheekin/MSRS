Opperly and Farquar are alike in their deep concern for the moral and ethical implications of science in a post-apocalyptic world, and both believe that the Thinkers' technological advancements—particularly Maizie—are based on deception rather than truth. They share a skepticism toward the idea that advanced technology can deliver genuine wisdom or peace without moral cost. However, they differ fundamentally in their approach: Opperly advocates for restraint, wisdom, and the recognition that violence and radical action are not solutions to systemic problems. He sees history as shaped by human choices, not just by power or deception, and believes that scientists must avoid becoming instruments of chaos. In contrast, Farquar is driven by anger and a desire for confrontation, believing that the Thinkers are vulnerable and must be actively challenged through exposure and aggression. He sees their power as fragile and based on illusion, and insists on a direct struggle to expose and dismantle them. While both are critical of the Thinkers, Opperly seeks balance and understanding, whereas Farquar demands a violent reckoning—reflecting a broader moral divide between pacifist reflection and revolutionary action in a world where truth is scarce and power is ambiguous.
