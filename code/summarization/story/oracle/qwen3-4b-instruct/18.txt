Janis's character is significant because her personal account of licking a stamp becomes the crucial clue that reveals the true nature of the epidemic. While the story initially presents a mysterious, widespread illness affecting various groups—writers, artists, office workers, and college students—Janis’s detailed recollection of her routine, including the act of writing and mailing a letter to her mother, highlights a specific behavioral pattern. This behavior, uncommon among government workers or those in large offices (who use automated systems), points to a key difference in exposure. The lab’s eventual identification of the culprit—contaminated adhesive on four-cent stamps—demonstrates that the illness is transmitted through contact with the stamp’s stickum. Janis, as a representative of the affected group, embodies the transmission vector: individuals who handle stamps personally, especially women in small offices or creative professions. Her vulnerability and fear underscore the personal, human impact of the outbreak, transforming a cold epidemiological mystery into a story of individual agency and discovery. Without her narrative, the central pattern would remain elusive, making Janis’s role pivotal in both solving the mystery and humanizing the crisis.
