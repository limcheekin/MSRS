Janis's character is significant because her symptoms and personal account provide a crucial, human-centered insight into the mysterious epidemic. Her description of fever, dizziness, and fear mirrors the reported symptoms of the outbreak, suggesting a possible connection between the illness and everyday exposures. Most importantly, her confession of writing and mailing a letter to her mother—something seemingly mundane—becomes a pivotal clue. Andy’s discovery of her letter and subsequent investigation reveal that the epidemic may not be purely biological but could involve psychological or communicative elements. Her actions hint at the spread of information or fear, reinforcing the idea that the disease might be transmitted through emotional or social contagion rather than physical contact. Additionally, Janis’s vulnerability and fear reflect the broader societal anxiety surrounding the outbreak, grounding the story in human emotion. Her presence thus shifts the narrative from a cold scientific inquiry to one of personal experience and psychological impact, making her a vital anchor in understanding how the epidemic spreads not just through bodies, but through trust, communication, and fear.
