The narrator in *I, the Unspeakable* by Walt Sheldon is not directly aware of the enigmatic voice's true identity or origin, but he experiences it as a persistent, seductive force within his dreams. The voice urges him to defy societal norms by suggesting he change his name and take a radical step—defying the rigid eugenic system that governs reproduction and identity in his world. Though the narrator initially dismisses the voice as a product of sleep or psychological disturbance, it grows increasingly compelling and personal. The repeated plea—"Do it, please—for me"—implies a deep, almost emotional connection between the narrator and the voice. However, the narrator remains uncertain whether the voice is external, internalized, or even a manifestation of his own suppressed desire for freedom. The relationship is thus one of psychological and moral tension: the narrator is both repelled by and drawn to the voice’s call to rebellion. Ultimately, the voice acts as a symbolic force representing the internal conflict between conformity and self-determination, and the narrator's struggle with it reflects a deeper yearning to break free from institutional control.
