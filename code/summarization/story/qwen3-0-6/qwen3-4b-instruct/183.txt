The Kumaji's role in the story is central to the exploration of betrayal, survival, and the complexities of homecoming. As nomadic warriors who view the colonial settlement as a threat to their way of life, the Kumaji represent both external danger and a mirror to the moral ambiguities of loyalty and identity. Their presence forces Steve and Mary into acts of violence and deception—such as Steve killing Kumaji guards and lying about his father’s death—to survive. These actions underscore the theme of betrayal, not only of trust among allies but also of personal integrity. The Kumaji also symbolize the inescapable reality of conflict in a world where survival demands compromise. When Steve ultimately confronts the truth—that his father Tobias Whiting betrayed the community to secure wealth by joining the Kumaji—his journey becomes one of reckoning with familial betrayal and moral failure. Yet, the eventual homecoming of the caravan, marked by warmth and solidarity despite trauma, suggests that true belonging is not defined by innocence or purity, but by resilience, forgiveness, and shared hardship. In this way, the Kumaji serve as both antagonists and catalysts, illuminating the emotional and ethical dimensions of return to community after betrayal.
