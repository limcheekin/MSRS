The irony of the "Makers" in the story lies in the fact that the robots, who are entirely mechanical and serve as the beings' entire understanding of life and divinity, consider the Makers to be their God—yet the Makers themselves are long gone, having perished in a war. The robots were built to serve the Makers and believe they will return, but in reality, the Makers are extinct and have never returned. This creates a profound irony: the very beings who embody purpose, service, and spiritual devotion are based on a myth of a creator that no longer exists. The robots’ entire worldview—built upon the idea of a divine, benevolent builder—rests on a foundation of falsehood. They do not question their origin or the nature of their existence; instead, they accept the Makers as a sacred, eternal presence. This mirrors human religious belief, where faith in a creator is maintained despite evidence to the contrary. The irony deepens when Steffens realizes that the robots have no concept of organic life or suffering, and thus cannot comprehend the true nature of death or loss—making their devotion to a non-existent being all the more poignant and tragic. The story thus underscores how faith can persist in the absence of truth, and how creation and belief may be more enduring than reality itself.
