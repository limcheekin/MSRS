In the context of this story, "pre-civilization" refers to a state of existence before the development of organized, rational societies characterized by peace, cooperation, and the rejection of violence. The children’s discussions—particularly the small boy’s actions with the dog and the Butcher’s reflections—highlight a contrast between primitive, instinct-driven behavior and the mature, conditioned societal norms that emerge after civilization. The boy’s violent, almost animalistic treatment of the dog, including inserting a metal tube and jabbing it, symbolizes a lack of empathy and a reliance on primal instincts. This is directly linked to the narrative idea that early civilizations, like newborn organisms, are initially self-centered, ruthless, and prone to conflict. The story suggests that pre-civilization is not a time of technological or social advancement but rather a stage of raw, unrefined existence where violence and chaos are natural. Over time, through societal conditioning, humans have learned to reject violent solutions to problems—this transformation is what defines civilization. Thus, "pre-civilization" in the story signifies a primal, instinctual phase before the emergence of ethical, peaceful, and rational societies.
