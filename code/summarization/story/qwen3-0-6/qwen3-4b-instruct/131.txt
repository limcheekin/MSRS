John Smith is interested in holes because they are central to his theory about the structure of time and history. He believes that all cultures are built upon the interconnected histories of various time dimensions, and that holes—both physical and metaphysical—serve as gateways or portals through which one can observe or access these dimensions firsthand. When John falls through a hole in the ground while returning to the university, he loses contact with his own time dimension, fearing that without retrieving himself, he will be discredited for failing to prove his theory about pre-tape musical instruments. His obsession with holes stems from the belief that each hole represents a potential entry point into another time or reality, allowing direct observation of ancient history. This makes the study of holes not just a physical phenomenon, but a vital method for understanding the fabric of existence. Thus, John’s fascination with holes is both scientific and existential—he sees them as essential to his mission of proving that music existed before recorded media, and that time is not linear but interwoven through dimensional holes.
