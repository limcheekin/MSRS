Farquar serves as a critical counterpoint to Opperly’s moral and philosophical stance, embodying the tension between radical action and ethical restraint in the narrative. While Opperly advocates for a non-violent, intellectually grounded approach—arguing that power is rooted in conscience, not force—he sees the Thinkers as vulnerable charlatans who rely on illusion and fear. Farquar, in contrast, is driven by a fierce desire to expose and dismantle the Thinkers, believing their power is built on delusion and instability. His anger and insistence on confrontation reflect a belief that moral clarity requires active resistance, even if it means violence or upheaval. This clash underscores the central conflict: is true morality found in peaceful revelation or in decisive action against deception? Farquar’s presence amplifies the story’s exploration of power dynamics, showing how different ideologies—scientific integrity versus ideological manipulation—shape the future. His unrelenting drive to act highlights the danger of moral absolutism, while also challenging Opperly’s idealism. Ultimately, Farquar’s role is not just as a character but as a symbol of the destructive potential of unchecked ambition, illustrating that power without moral grounding can only perpetuate cycles of fear and control.
