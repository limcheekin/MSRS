Opperly and Farquar are alike in their deep concern for the moral and ethical implications of advanced technology and their shared awareness of the dangers posed by unverified scientific claims, particularly those of the Thinkers. Both men recognize the power of belief and illusion in times of crisis, with Opperly emphasizing how people turn to "magicians" during hardship, while Farquar sees the Thinkers as charlatans whose influence is rooted in fear and deception. They both view the current era as one of moral ambiguity, where truth and manipulation are intertwined.

Yet they differ fundamentally in their responses. Opperly embodies a weary, reflective caution, having lived through periods of scientific fear and despair, and believes that violence and confrontation only deepen suffering. He advocates restraint, emphasizing the need for scientists to avoid entanglement in power struggles. Farquar, in contrast, is passionate and confrontational, convinced that moral clarity requires active resistance—believing that the Thinkers are vulnerable and will eventually seek peace through negotiation, which he sees as a necessary act of strength. While Opperly seeks balance and historical understanding, Farquar embraces action and personal resolve, reflecting a more aggressive stance on confronting technological and ideological threats.
