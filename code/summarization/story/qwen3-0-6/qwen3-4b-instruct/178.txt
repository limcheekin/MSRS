The strange aspect of this harsh and rapidly aging world is that life is not only fleeting but compressed into an extremely short span—eight days from birth to death—where individuals age and die at an accelerated, almost unnatural pace. This is not a gradual decline but a violent, rapid cycle: childhood, adolescence, and manhood occur in moments, and old age arrives swiftly, leaving little time for normal human experiences. The world’s inhabitants are shaped by extreme environmental pressures—such as solar radiation and harsh climates—that speed up biological aging. The narrative reveals that this condition originated from a catastrophic crash of alien metal seeds on a barren planet, where survivors were subjected to intense radiation and physical transformation, resulting in a life cycle measured in weeks rather than decades. Even the natural world reflects this urgency—avalanches occur daily, plant life blooms and dies in moments, and the environment itself is in constant flux. The emotional and existential toll is profound: characters struggle with the impossibility of forming deep bonds or memories, as time is too short for love, reflection, or growth. Thus, what is strange is not just the brevity of life, but the complete reversal of human experience—where survival, awareness, and emotion must be achieved in a fraction of a normal lifespan, making existence feel like a desperate, fleeting act of defiance against entropy.
