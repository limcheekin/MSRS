The plot of *The Master of Life and Death* centers on Roy Walton, an assistant administrator of the Bureau of Population Equalization (Popeek), a futuristic government agency tasked with controlling Earth's overpopulation. As Earth's population reaches seven billion and resources dwindle, Popeek enforces strict measures to maintain balance, including the euthanasia of "substandard" individuals—such as children deemed genetically unfit or adults suffering from diseases like tuberculosis—and the forced relocation of populations to remote areas like Patagonia. Walton’s role is deeply moral: he must decide whether to uphold the system or break from it. When a grieving poet, Mr. Prior, arrives at his office demanding that his newborn son be spared from Happysleep, Walton is confronted with the human cost of the policy. Though he initially refuses, the emotional weight of Prior’s plea—especially given Prior's own history of tuberculosis—triggers a personal crisis. The story explores the moral ambiguity of state-enforced population control, questioning whether such measures are just or humane. Ultimately, Walton makes the decision to spare one child, a small but symbolic rebellion against the system. This act forces him to confront the core dilemma: can a bureaucrat uphold the law while also recognizing the intrinsic value of human life? The narrative ultimately reflects on the tension between societal survival and individual dignity in a world facing existential crisis.
